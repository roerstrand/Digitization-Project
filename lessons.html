Reflection

Five things the project made clear

  1. 1
    Metadata requires planning before digitization begins.

    Deciding what to record after the scanning is done means going back — to the archive, to the scanner, to ambiguous decisions already made. A schema agreed on in advance costs an afternoon; retrofitting one costs far more.

  2. 2
    Digitization is as much about context as preservation.

    A scan without provenance is just a picture. What makes a digitized object useful is knowing where it came from, when it was captured, and what it shows. Stripping context in the name of efficiency produces collections that age poorly.

  3. 3
    Public accessibility depends on information structure.

    Publishing raw files is not the same as making material accessible. A structured presentation — consistent fields, stable identifiers, readable licenses — is what allows someone outside the project to actually use what has been made.

  4. 4
    Digital preservation requires sustainable documentation.

    Files without documentation are at risk of becoming uninterpretable. The surrogate numbering system, the shelf marks, the scanning parameters — these are not administrative overhead. They are what keeps the collection meaningful after the project team is gone.

  5. 5
    Scope is a decision, not a limitation.

    The Baltic Exhibition is a large topic. Constraining the project to four buildings was not a compromise — it was what made depth possible. A tightly scoped collection done thoroughly is more useful than a broad one done superficially.
