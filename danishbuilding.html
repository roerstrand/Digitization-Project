The Danish House

Denmark's participation in the Baltic Exhibition was taken seriously as a matter of national prestige. A design competition was held among prominent Danish architects, offering significant prizes for the best proposal to represent the country. Henning Hansen emerged victorious, and his winning design drew on the tradition of the Danish Sjælland manor house — a deliberately vernacular choice, grounding Denmark's contribution in regional identity rather than cosmopolitan fashion.

The building featured red brick walls, a tiled roof, and a two-storey façade with square-paned windows. Its most dramatic element was the central Renaissance-style gate, approached across a decorative moat. The gatework was carved from sandstone quarried on the island of Bornholm and shipped to Malmö — a detail that underscored the importance of authentic Danish materials to the project's conception.

Inside, the Danish pavilion displayed applied arts, crafts, and industrial goods. Denmark had long cultivated a reputation for refined decorative tradition, and the exhibition was an opportunity to showcase that character to Nordic and European visitors alike. The building was widely admired in the press on both sides of Öresund.

Behind the scenes, the construction period was not without tension. A Danish master craftsman named Nielsen was found to be paying his workers at nearly half the rate mandated by Swedish trade unions. The dispute briefly halted work and drew attention to the friction between Danish and Swedish labor norms. It was ultimately resolved by applying Swedish law — a reminder that despite the short crossing from Copenhagen, the fairgrounds were firmly on Swedish soil.

Like all the national pavilions, the Danish building was torn down after the exhibition closed in October 1914. Its physical traces exist only in photographs, catalogs, and contemporary press reporting — the documentary record this project seeks to preserve.

Architectural Style

Skønvirke: A Danish Jugend

Denmark developed its own variant of the Jugend aesthetic under the name Skønvirke — literally "beautiful work" — which placed particular emphasis on the decorative arts, craft quality, and a connection to Nordic vernacular tradition. Unlike the more architecturally driven Jugend movements in Germany or Austria, Skønvirke was rooted in the applied arts: ceramics, silverwork, textiles, and furniture. The Danish pavilion expressed this tradition through its materials and detailing — Bornholm sandstone, carved ornamental gateways, and hand-finished surfaces — rather than through dramatic architectural gestures.

National Identity

Vernacular Form as Cultural Argument

Henning Hansen's winning design made a deliberate architectural argument: that Denmark's cultural identity was rooted in its regional rural tradition rather than in cosmopolitan fashion. The Sjælland manor house — with its red brick walls, pitched tile roof, and courtyard plan — was chosen as the formal reference not because it was modern, but because it was authentically Danish. This strategy of deploying vernacular forms as a vehicle of national representation was common across Scandinavian participation in international exhibitions from the late nineteenth century onward, and the Danish pavilion stands as a particularly clear example of it.

Labor and Law

A Dispute on Swedish Soil

The construction of the Danish pavilion was briefly halted by a labor dispute that threw the difference between Danish and Swedish working norms into sharp relief. A Danish master craftsman named Nielsen was found to be paying his workers at roughly half the rate required under Swedish union agreements. The matter was taken up by Swedish labor organizations and ultimately resolved in favor of applying Swedish standards — since the fairgrounds were Swedish soil. The episode, documented in the contemporary press, was a small but telling drama: national exhibitions competed diplomatically on the surface while exposing practical frictions beneath.
