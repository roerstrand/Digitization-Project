Baltic Exhibition · Malmö 1914 · Denmark

Danish House

Henning Hansen · Sjælland manor · 1914

Denmark's participation in the Baltic Exhibition was taken seriously as a matter of national prestige. The Danish committee announced an architectural competition open to every Danish architect, academically trained or not, with four prizes from 300 to 800 kronor. In February 1913 a jury in Copenhagen — joined by Ferdinand Boberg — awarded first prize to Henning Hansen, whose design drew on the tradition of the Zealand manor house: a deliberately vernacular choice, grounding Denmark's contribution in regional identity rather than cosmopolitan fashion.

The house’s story

Brick, towers and a portal

The building had red brick walls, a red tiled roof and low hexagonal corner towers, with a large Renaissance portal at the centre of the façade, reached by a bridge over a water-filled moat. The portal was first meant to be cut from basalt from the Faroe Islands, but fearing the stone would not arrive in time, the committee switched to Bornholm sandstone, carved by the stonemasons E. Nielsen of Copenhagen.

Inside the pavilion

Inside, the Danish pavilion displayed applied arts, crafts, and industrial goods. Demand was so great that the press reported in October 1913 that the building could not hold every exhibitor who had applied. A Malmö paper visiting in March 1914 found the house rising "homely and dignified", its small white windows shining around Denmark's arms in sandstone above the main portal.

Demolition and the record

Like all the national pavilions, the Danish building was torn down after the exhibition closed in October 1914. Its physical traces exist only in the catalogs and photographs at Malmö City Library and the newspaper clippings at Malmö City Archive — the documentary record this project digitized, and the source for everything told on this page.

Architectural Style

Skønvirke: A Danish Jugend

Denmark developed its own variant of the Jugend aesthetic under the name Skønvirke — literally "beautiful work" — which placed particular emphasis on the decorative arts, craft quality, and a connection to Nordic vernacular tradition. Unlike the more architecturally driven Jugend movements in Germany or Austria, Skønvirke was rooted in the applied arts: ceramics, silverwork, textiles, and furniture. The Danish pavilion expressed this tradition through its materials and detailing — Bornholm sandstone, carved ornamental gateways, and hand-finished surfaces — rather than through dramatic architectural gestures.

National Identity

Vernacular Form as Cultural Argument

Henning Hansen's winning design made a deliberate architectural argument: that Denmark's cultural identity was rooted in its regional rural tradition rather than in cosmopolitan fashion. The Sjælland manor house — with its red brick walls, pitched tile roof, and courtyard plan — was chosen as the formal reference not because it was modern, but because it was authentically Danish. This strategy of deploying vernacular forms as a vehicle of national representation was common across Scandinavian participation in international exhibitions from the late nineteenth century onward, and the Danish pavilion stands as a particularly clear example of it.

Labor and Law

A Dispute on Swedish Soil

In November 1913 the timber work on the Danish pavilion came to a standstill. Master carpenter Nielsen from Aarhus had brought 13–14 Danish carpenters to Malmö on their Aarhus hourly rates. The Malmö union demanded that they join it and that the contract, agreed at 8,000 kronor, be raised by at least 6,000 kronor — almost double. The Danish workers laid down their tools. Three weeks later work was still stopped: the Danish general commissioner ruled out a larger state grant, the builder said he could not meet the union's demand without exceeding the budget, and Danish exhibitors feared losing up to a third of their floor space. The Danish committee opened direct talks with the union in Malmö. The episode is followed week by week in the newspaper clippings digitized for this project — a small but telling drama: national exhibitions competed diplomatically on the surface while exposing practical frictions beneath.
