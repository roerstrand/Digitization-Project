The German Hall

Germany’s contribution to the Baltic Exhibition was the largest in scale and among the most lavishly appointed. Budgeted at 100,000 mark and covering 16,000 square metres of exhibition space, the German pavilion was a statement of imperial cultural confidence. The overall design was led by Hans Alfred Richter, and the building combined the imposing presence of the Wilhelmine Wertheim commercial style with Jugend ornamentation.

The structure was two storeys tall, 75 metres long and 15 metres wide, with a Mansard roof, vaulted vestibules, and a system of arcades encircling a central open courtyard. At the heart of that courtyard stood a decorative fountain clad in majolica tiles, designed by Alfred Grenander — a Swedish architect then based in Berlin who was already gaining recognition for his work on the Berlin U-Bahn. The contrast between Grenander’s precise geometric Jugend sensibility and the heavier imperial framework of the building reflected the broader tension within German design culture at the time.

The interior exhibitions were rich in prestige objects. Among the highlights were artefacts loaned from the personal collection of Emperor Wilhelm II, displayed alongside applied arts, industrial goods, and examples of German craftwork. Germany’s aim was clear: to project power and cultural sophistication in equal measure.

Construction was not without cost. One worker died after falling 16 metres from the scaffolding during the build phase — a fatality recorded in contemporary accounts but largely overshadowed by the spectacle of the finished structure. The summer of 1914 would bring far darker news: on 7 May, while the exhibition was open, a German naval vessel docked in Malmö harbour to collect German nationals recalled for military service. Within weeks, Germany was at war. The pavilion that had been constructed as a cultural bridge became, retrospectively, a last flourish before catastrophe.

The building was dismantled following the exhibition’s close in October 1914, leaving only photographic and printed records — a portion of which are presented here.
