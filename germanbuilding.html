Baltic Exhibition · Malmö 1914 · Germany

German Hall

Wilhelmine pavilions · Hans Alfred Richter · 1914

Germany’s contribution to the Baltic Exhibition was the largest in scale and among the most lavishly appointed. Budgeted at 100,000 mark and covering 16,000 square metres of exhibition space, the German pavilion was a statement of imperial cultural confidence. The overall design was led by Hans Alfred Richter, and the building combined the imposing presence of the Wilhelmine Wertheim commercial style with Jugend ornamentation.

The hall’s story

The structure was two storeys tall, 75 metres long and 15 metres wide, with a Mansard roof, vaulted vestibules, and a system of arcades encircling a central open courtyard. At the heart of that courtyard stood a decorative fountain clad in majolica tiles, designed by Alfred Grenander — a Swedish architect then based in Berlin who was already gaining recognition for his work on the Berlin U-Bahn. The contrast between Grenander’s precise geometric Jugend sensibility and the heavier imperial framework of the building reflected the broader tension within German design culture at the time.

The interior exhibitions were rich in prestige objects. Among the highlights were artefacts loaned from the personal collection of Emperor Wilhelm II, displayed alongside applied arts, industrial goods, and examples of German craftwork. Germany’s aim was clear: to project power and cultural sophistication in equal measure.

Construction was not without cost. In October 1913 a worker named Lindén fell 16 metres inside the German hall and was killed under falling roof trusses and beams; his colleagues refused to resume work until proper safety arrangements were made. In March 1914 a scaffold in the hall's Kejsarsalen collapsed under a load of plaster ornaments, badly injuring the stucco worker Karl Pettersson. Both accidents survive only in the newspaper clippings at Malmö City Archive.

The summer of 1914 brought far darker news. In the first days of August, while the exhibition was still open, a German warship anchored off Malmö to collect members of the German section recalled for military service — reported in the press on 7 August. The pavilion that had been built as a cultural bridge became, in retrospect, a last flourish before catastrophe.

The building was dismantled following the exhibition’s close in October 1914, leaving only photographic and printed records. The account on this page is drawn from them: the catalogs at Malmö City Library, and the newspaper clippings digitized at Malmö City Archive.

Architectural Style

Jugendstil Between Two Worlds

Germany's experience of Jugendstil was defined by a tension between its Munich origins and its Berlin development. The Munich variant — centered around the journal Jugend, founded in 1896 — embraced organic naturalism, botanical motifs, and a utopian craft ideal. The Berlin school, operating in the context of rapid industrialization and imperial ambition, moved toward a more disciplined, geometric application of the style. The German Hall embodied this division: Richter's external framework carried the weight of Wilhelmine imperial representation, while Alfred Grenander's courtyard fountain introduced a more precise and modern decorative language.

Alfred Grenander

The Architect of the Inner Courtyard

Alfred Grenander (1863–1931) was Swedish by birth but had built his reputation in Berlin, where he became the principal designer of stations for the growing U-Bahn network. His work on the Berlin underground is now recognized as a significant body of German Jugend design — rationalized, functional, yet richly ornamented. His contribution to the German Hall's inner courtyard brought that sensibility to Malmö: the arcaded walkways, the majolica-tiled fountain, and the medallion ornaments along the colonnade all bore his characteristic balance between decoration and structural clarity. It was a rare instance of a Swedish architect representing Germany on an international stage.

Cultural Diplomacy

A Statement of Imperial Culture

Germany's participation was conceived from the outset as a matter of cultural prestige. The budget of 100,000 mark, the 16,000 square metres of exhibition space, and the loans from the personal collection of Emperor Wilhelm II were all signals of a state that understood international exhibitions as a form of diplomacy. The pavilion was intended to demonstrate that German cultural and industrial production stood at the forefront of European civilization. Within weeks of the opening, that claim would be tested in a very different arena — and the pavilion that had been constructed as a cultural bridge became, retrospectively, a last flourish before catastrophe.

The German hall in the newspapers

  1. 7 Apr 1913

    Tyskland på Baltiska utställningen

    Architect Hans Alfred Richter and general commissioner Mathies arrive in Malmö. No less than 15,000 m² is set aside for Germany, and the imperial majolica works at Cadinen are to exhibit.

    Vol. Ö II6, p. 30
  2. 17 Oct 1913

    Tysklands paviljong snart färdig

    A two-storey building in the Wertheim style with a mansard roof, 75 metres long and 15 wide. Its forecourt fountain in majolica is designed by the Swedish architect Alfred Grenander in Berlin.

    Vol. Ö II1, p. 61
  3. 17 Oct 1913

    Ett dödande fall vid Baltiska utställningens bygge

    The worker Lindén falls 16 metres inside the German hall and is killed; work stops until safety is improved.

    Vol. Ö II5, p. 16
  4. 27 Nov 1913

    Tyskland och Baltiska utställningen

    The German imperial government is to ask the Reichstag for 100,000 marks for Germany's representation in Malmö.

    Vol. Ö II1, p. 92
  5. 20 Mar 1914

    Svår olyckshändelse vid Baltiska utställningen

    Det tyska utställningsgodset
