The Russian Pavilion

Russia’s participation in the Baltic Exhibition was, from the beginning, complicated by diplomatic and logistical hesitation. Initial planning had included a substantial Russian commissariat — an official body responsible for coordinating exhibits and representing the country’s cultural and industrial output. The original architectural concept came from M.M. Periatkowitsch, a prominent Russian architect known for his work in St. Petersburg.

As the exhibition date approached, the Russian commissariat withdrew its formal participation. The reasons were partly administrative and partly political: the early months of 1914 brought mounting tension across Europe, and Russia’s leadership had other preoccupations. The withdrawal left the question of whether Russia would appear at all.

It was Ferdinand Boberg — the Swedish architect who had also designed the Baltic Tower and the exhibition’s master plan — who stepped in to complete the Russian pavilion. Working in consultation with remaining Russian contacts, Boberg produced a building that preserved a Russian architectural character while fitting within the broader visual harmony of the fairgrounds. His involvement ensured that a Russian presence was visible to the exhibition’s estimated 1.3 million visitors, even if the official machinery behind it had largely retreated.

The result was a building that occupied an ambiguous position: architecturally Russian, administratively absent. What was shown inside — to the extent that exhibits were assembled at all — reflected the reduced scope of the participation. The Russian pavilion stands, in retrospect, as an early sign of the dislocations that would define the rest of 1914. By August, Russia was at war. By October, the exhibition had closed. The pavilion, like all the others, was torn down.

Given the limited photographic documentation of the Russian building’s interior, this project has chosen to focus its attention on the other three national pavilions. A single exterior image is presented here as a record of the building’s appearance.

Architectural Style

Russian Architecture and the European Fin-de-Siècle

Russian architecture at the turn of the twentieth century developed along two overlapping tracks. One engaged with the international Art Nouveau movement — known in Russia as Modern — which shaped buildings in St. Petersburg and Moscow through sinuous forms and organic surface decoration. The other drew on a distinctly Russian heritage: the Neo-Russian or Russian Revival movement, which looked to medieval Muscovite churches, Byzantine domes, and folk ornamental traditions for its vocabulary. The pavilion at the Baltic Exhibition drew primarily on the latter, presenting to Nordic visitors an architecture of onion domes and Oriental-inflected ornament that marked Russia as culturally distinct from its Scandinavian and German neighbors.

Periatkowitsch and Boberg

A Pavilion by Two Architects

The original design for the Russian pavilion was attributed to M.M. Periatkowitsch, an architect active in St. Petersburg and known within the Russian architectural establishment. As Russia's formal commissariat withdrew from active participation, the responsibility for completing the building fell to Ferdinand Boberg — the Swedish architect who had already designed the Baltic Tower and the entire exhibition layout. Boberg's involvement introduced a pragmatic synthesis: a building that retained its Russian visual character while conforming to the practical requirements of the fairgrounds. The result was architecturally coherent but institutionally ambiguous.

Politics and Presence

An Ambiguous Presence

The Russian pavilion occupied a paradoxical position at the exhibition. Architecturally, it was among the most visually distinctive structures on the grounds — the twin onion domes were visible from a great distance and formed a striking contrast with the Scandinavian and Germanic buildings nearby. Yet administratively, Russia had largely withdrawn. The exhibits inside were limited; the official machinery of the commissariat had retreated. By August 1914, the paradox had deepened: the country whose pavilion stood on the fairgrounds was at war with Germany, whose pavilion stood fifty metres away. The shared visual space of the exhibition had become, briefly, a map of a continent at war with itself.
